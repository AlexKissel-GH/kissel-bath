Short answer: sometimes yes, sometimes no — and the line is based on what you're changing, not how much you're spending. If your project touches plumbing lines, electrical circuits, or structure, a **bathroom remodel permit in Denver** is almost certainly required. If you're swapping finishes and fixtures in place, it usually isn't.

We're a licensed and insured General Contractor based in Aurora, and permits are part of our everyday work across Denver, Aurora, Lakewood, and the rest of the metro. Here's how to figure out where your project falls.

## Work That Usually Does NOT Need a Permit

In most Denver-metro jurisdictions, you can typically do the following without pulling a permit:

- Painting, wallpaper, and cosmetic finishes
- Replacing flooring (tile, LVP) without structural changes
- Swapping a vanity, toilet, faucet, or light fixture in the same location
- Replacing a tub or shower surround on existing plumbing in the same footprint
- New countertops, mirrors, and accessories

Our Surrounds Only Facelift (from $6,500, one day) is a good example — new wall panels over properly prepped existing surfaces, fixtures in place. That's cosmetic work and generally permit-free.

## Work That Usually DOES Need a Permit

The rule of thumb across the metro: if you're modifying the systems behind the walls, the city wants to inspect it.

- **Plumbing changes** — moving a drain, converting a tub drain to a shower drain with relocation, adding a second sink, rerouting supply lines
- **Electrical changes** — new circuits, moving outlets, adding a dedicated circuit for a bidet seat or heated floor, moving lighting over a shower (which has its own code requirements)
- **Structural changes** — removing or altering walls, even non-load-bearing in some jurisdictions
- **Ventilation** — adding or relocating an exhaust fan duct to the exterior
- **Windows** — changing the size of an opening

Note the nuance on tub-to-shower work: replacing a tub with a shower using the existing drain location is often permit-free, but many conversions benefit from upsizing the 1½" tub drain to a code-preferred 2" shower drain, and once you're altering drain lines, most departments want a plumbing permit and inspection.

## How It Works in Denver Specifically

In the City and County of Denver, residential permits run through **Denver Community Planning & Development**, and many trade permits can be applied for online through the city's e-permits system. Plumbing and electrical permits are typically pulled by the licensed trade contractors doing the work. After rough-in work is complete — pipes and wires in place but before walls close — an inspector visits to verify it, then a final inspection happens when the job is done.

Timeline-wise, trade permits for a bathroom are usually quick; they're not the months-long process people associate with additions or new construction. The inspection scheduling adds a day or two of coordination at most on a typical bathroom.

## Other Metro Cities Have Their Own Rules

If you're outside Denver proper, your project goes through your own city's building department, and the details differ:

- **Aurora** runs its own building division with online permitting; requirements are broadly similar to Denver's.
- **Lakewood, Arvada, Littleton, Centennial, Parker, Highlands Ranch (Douglas County)** — each has its own department, fee schedule, and inspection process. Unincorporated areas go through the county.
- **HOAs are a separate layer.** In Highlands Ranch and much of Centennial and Parker, an HOA architectural review may apply to exterior changes — less common for bathrooms, but worth checking if a window or vent changes.

This patchwork is one reason to hire a contractor who works across the metro regularly. Fees, submittal requirements, and inspection lead times differ enough that "how it worked on my last house in Arvada" isn't a reliable guide for your place in Aurora.

## The Real Risks of Skipping the Permit

Homeowners sometimes ask whether they can just skip it. Here's what that gamble actually involves:

- **Stop-work orders and fines** if a neighbor reports the work or an inspector notices it
- **Opening finished walls** so an inspector can verify plumbing and electrical after the fact — at your cost
- **Insurance problems** if a leak or fire traces back to unpermitted work; claims can be contested
- **Resale friction.** Unpermitted work surfaces in buyer inspections and appraisals; in the Denver metro's competitive market, it's a common reason deals get renegotiated or die
- **No independent verification** that the work behind your new tile is safe and to code — the thing the permit was paying for

None of this is scaremongering; it's the standard list of problems we get called in to fix on DIY and handyman jobs.

## Why the Contractor Should Pull the Permit

One red flag worth knowing: if a contractor asks you to pull the permit yourself "as the homeowner," walk away. The permit holder is legally responsible for the work. When we pull it, our license and insurance stand behind the job, we schedule the inspections, and the record of permitted work belongs to your house — which is worth something when you sell.

We're also an EPA Lead-Safe Certified Firm (NAT-F212803-1), which matters on permits too: demolition in pre-1978 homes — a huge share of Denver bungalows and Aurora ranches — has federal containment requirements that inspectors and buyers increasingly ask about.
