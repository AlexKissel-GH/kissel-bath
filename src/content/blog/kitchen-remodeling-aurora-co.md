## Aurora's Housing Stock Shapes Your Kitchen Project

Aurora grew in waves, and each wave built a different kitchen:

- **1950s–70s Aurora** — the ranches and split-levels of Hoffman Heights, Del Mar, Mission Viejo, and the streets around Heather Gardens. These are the classic metro ranch kitchens: galley or closed L-shape, 8×10 to 10×12 feet, a wall between the kitchen and dining room, soffits over the cabinets. Great bones, dated rooms.
- **1980s–90s Aurora** — bigger two-stories and tri-levels pushing south and east. Kitchens got larger but were often built with builder-grade oak cabinets and laminate counters that are now past their lifespan.
- **2000s and newer** — Tallyn's Reach, Southshore, and the southeast corridor. Open plans already; the work here is usually upgrading finishes rather than fixing layouts.

The practical difference: in older Aurora, most projects involve opening sightlines and modernizing systems. In newer Aurora, the layout usually works and the money goes into counters, cabinets, and finishes. ## Permits in Aurora: The E-Permits System

Aurora homeowners have it relatively easy. The City of Aurora runs an e-permits online portal, and trade permits for straightforward kitchen work — like-for-like plumbing fixture moves, added circuits — typically process quickly. Structural work, like removing the wall between your kitchen and dining room, goes through plan review and takes longer.

What needs a permit in Aurora:

- Moving or adding plumbing lines
- New circuits or panel work
- Removing or altering any wall (structural review)
- Gas line changes for a range relocation

What usually doesn't: painting, cabinet refacing, countertop replacement in place, new hardware, or swapping appliances in existing locations.

One more older-Aurora note: if your home was built before 1978, lead paint is a possibility during demo. We're certified as an EPA Lead-Safe Firm (NAT-F212803-1) and follow federal containment and cleanup practices on every older home — ask any contractor you interview whether they can say the same, because the law requires it.

## Layout Moves That Work in Aurora Ranch Homes

After years of walking Aurora ranches, these are the conversions that consistently work:

- **Open the kitchen to the dining room.** The single most requested change in 1960s Aurora. Usually means an engineered LVL beam — most ranch dividing walls are load-bearing — plus a permit, but the result transforms the whole main floor.
- **Peninsula instead of island.** Many Aurora ranch kitchens are 10–11 feet wide — too narrow for a proper island with 42-inch aisles. A peninsula off the opened wall gives the seating without the squeeze.
- **Remove the soffits.** Bulkheads above the cabinets were built-in dust shelves. Running cabinets to the ceiling adds a full shelf of storage and makes low ranch ceilings feel taller.
- **Vent the range outside.** Many original Aurora kitchens have recirculating hoods. Ducting to the exterior is a modest cost that matters for resale and for your indoor air.

## Practical Details Specific to Aurora Homes

A few things we plan for on every local kitchen:

- **Dry climate.** Colorado's low humidity shrinks wood. New cabinets and hardwood flooring need to acclimate inside your home before installation, or you'll see gapped joints by the first winter.
- **Hard water.** Aurora's water is hard enough to spot glassware and wear cheap faucet finishes. We recommend fixtures with finishes that tolerate it, and quartz counters that never need sealing and won't etch.
- **Electrical capacity.** Modern kitchens need dedicated circuits for the dishwasher, disposal, microwave, and countertop receptacles. In 1960s Aurora that frequently means a panel conversation — better discovered at estimate than mid-demo.
- **Winter scheduling.** Aurora winters are mild enough that we remodel year-round, but material deliveries and your temporary-kitchen setup are easier if you know a January project means the grill is your backup stove in the cold.
