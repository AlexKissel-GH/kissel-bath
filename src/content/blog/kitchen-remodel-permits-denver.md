Here's the question that stalls more kitchen projects than any budget conversation: do I need a permit for this? The short answer for Denver homeowners — it depends entirely on what you're changing. Purely cosmetic work needs no permit at all. Electrical, plumbing, and structural changes always do. Getting this right matters more than most people realize, so let's walk through exactly where the lines are drawn in Denver, Aurora, and the surrounding metro.

## The Short Answer: Which Projects Need a Permit

| Project | Permit required? |
|---|---|
| Painting, hardware, backsplash | No |
| [Countertop replacement](/services/countertop-replacement-denver/) | No |
| [Cabinet refacing](/services/cabinet-refacing-denver/) or like-for-like replacement | No |
| New flooring | No |
| Adding outlets, new circuits, rewiring | Yes — electrical |
| Moving a sink, new supply or drain lines, gas line work | Yes — plumbing |
| Removing or altering a wall, adding a beam | Yes — structural/building |
| Replacing windows or exterior doors | Often — check locally |

The pattern is simple: if the work is surface-level, nobody needs to inspect it. If it involves systems that can start fires, flood your house, or compromise the structure, the city wants eyes on it. That's not bureaucracy for its own sake — those inspections are genuinely protective.

## How Permitting Works Across the Denver Metro

Each municipality runs its own building department, and the details vary:

- **Denver:** Permits go through Denver Community Planning & Development. Trade permits for straightforward electrical and plumbing work are often issued quickly; structural work requires plan review.
- **Aurora:** Aurora runs an e-permits online portal where licensed contractors apply for and track permits digitally — a genuinely convenient system for standard trade work.
- **Lakewood, Arvada, Centennial, Boulder, Highlands Ranch:** Each has its own building division (Highlands Ranch projects typically go through Douglas County). Requirements are similar in spirit — cosmetic work is exempt, trade and structural work is not — but fees, timelines, and submittal details differ.

If you live in an HOA community, add one more layer: the HOA's architectural approval is separate from city permitting, and you may need both.

## What the Permit Process Actually Looks Like

For a typical kitchen project involving trade work, the sequence is:

1. **Application.** Your contractor submits the permit application describing the scope — for structural changes, drawings or engineered plans may be required.
2. **Review and issuance.** Simple trade permits can issue in days; plan-reviewed structural permits take longer.
3. **Rough inspection.** An inspector checks the work while walls are still open — wiring, piping, framing — before anything gets closed up.
4. **Final inspection.** Once the project is complete, the inspector verifies everything is finished to code and closes the permit.

That closed permit becomes part of your home's public record — a real asset when you sell.

## What Happens If You Skip the Permit

We occasionally get called to fix projects where a previous contractor worked unpermitted. The consequences homeowners face:

- **Fines and stop-work orders** if the city discovers active unpermitted work
- **Tear-out requirements** — inspectors can require finished drywall to be opened so hidden work can be examined
- **Resale problems** — buyers' inspectors and appraisers flag unpermitted work, and it routinely becomes a negotiation point or a closing delay
- **Insurance exposure** — a fire or flood traced to unpermitted electrical or plumbing work can complicate or void a claim

The permit fee is a rounding error next to any of these outcomes.

## Permits and Older Denver Homes

Much of the metro's housing stock — 1950s–70s ranch homes in Aurora, Arvada, and Lakewood; brick bungalows near downtown — predates modern code by decades. That creates two permit-adjacent realities.

First, opening walls in these homes often reveals wiring or plumbing that no longer meets code. When permitted work touches those systems, inspectors may require the affected portions to be brought up to standard. It's frustrating mid-project, but it protects you.

Second, pre-1978 homes fall under federal lead-safe renovation rules. Any contractor disturbing painted surfaces in these homes must be an EPA Lead-Safe Certified Firm. We hold that certification (NAT-F212803-1) — it's a legal requirement for the work, and a meaningful safety issue for your family, not paperwork theater.

## Who Should Pull the Permit

Your contractor — full stop. When a licensed general contractor pulls the permit, they own code compliance, inspection scheduling, and the relationship with the building department. If a contractor asks you to pull a "homeowner permit" for work they're performing, treat it as a red flag: it transfers legal responsibility to you and often signals they can't pull permits themselves.
