Slippery tub walls, a 15-inch step to get in, nowhere to sit, and towel bars that people grab for balance even though they were never meant to hold body weight. If you or a parent plan to stay in your home for the long haul, the bathroom is the first room that needs to change.

**Walk-in shower installation in Denver** has become one of our most requested projects — and done right, it doesn't have to look like a hospital. Here's how to think about safety, cost, and design.

## What "Aging in Place" Actually Means for a Bathroom

Aging in place is simple: the home adapts to you, so you don't have to move when mobility changes. In a bathroom, that comes down to removing the three biggest injury risks — stepping over a tub wall, standing on a wet surface with nothing to hold, and having no way to bathe seated.

Falls are the leading cause of injury among older adults nationally, and bathrooms are where a large share of household falls happen. The good news is that the fixes are well understood, and most of them are easiest and cheapest to build in during a remodel rather than bolted on later.

## The Core Features of a Safe Walk-In Shower

A well-designed accessible shower combines several elements. Skipping one often undermines the others:

- **Low-threshold or curbless entry.** A low threshold of 2–4 inches works for most people. A true curbless, zero-entry design is the gold standard for walkers and wheelchairs — it requires recessing the floor framing or raising the surrounding floor, which adds carpentry, especially in the 1950s–70s ranch homes common across Aurora and Lakewood.
- **Slip-resistant flooring.** Smaller-format tile with more grout lines, or textured composite bases, give real traction when wet.
- **Grab bars — properly anchored.** Bars must be fastened into blocking or framing, never just drywall or tile. Even if you don't want bars today, we install blocking in the walls during the remodel so they can be added in an afternoon later.
- **A place to sit.** A built-in bench or a wall-mounted fold-down seat changes everything for anyone with balance or endurance issues.
- **Handheld showerhead on a slide bar.** Works standing or seated, and makes cleaning the shower itself easier.
- **Lighting and controls.** A light inside the shower zone, and a pressure-balanced valve with a lever handle — easier on arthritic hands and protected against scalding.
- **Clear floor space.** If a walker or wheelchair is in the picture now or later, the approach to the shower and the turning space in front of the vanity matter as much as the shower itself.

## Design That Doesn't Look Institutional

The fear we hear most: "I don't want my bathroom to look like a nursing home." It won't. The accessibility features that matter in 2026 are largely invisible:

- **Grab bars** now come in finishes that match your fixtures — matte black, brushed nickel, champagne bronze — and many double as shelves or towel bars while still being fully load-rated.
- **Curbless showers with a single fixed glass panel** are the most contemporary look in bathroom design right now, accessible or not.
- **Sentrel composite wall panels** give you the look of marble or stone with no grout lines. That's not just aesthetics: Colorado's hard water leaves mineral scale on grout and caulk, and scrubbing grout is exactly the kind of chore an accessible bathroom should eliminate. Sentrel carries a lifetime warranty.
- **Comfort-height toilets and floating or higher vanities** reduce bending without looking any different from standard fixtures.

Aging-in-place design, done well, reads as "nice bathroom." That's the goal.

## Planning Ahead in Denver's Older Housing Stock

A few local realities shape these projects:

- **Bungalow bathrooms are small.** In 1900s–1930s Denver bungalows, the typical bathroom is around 5×8 feet. Curbless entries and clearances take careful planning at that size, but a low-threshold conversion almost always fits.
- **Ranch homes have slabs.** In 1950s–70s ranches across Aurora, Centennial, and Littleton, drains are often set in concrete. Moving them for a curbless layout means cutting slab — doable, but it should be priced into the estimate, not discovered mid-job.
- **Pre-1978 homes require lead-safe demolition.** We're an EPA Lead-Safe Certified Firm (NAT-F212803-1), so containment and cleanup are built into our process.
- **Do it once.** The most expensive accessible bathroom is the one you remodel twice. If mobility is likely to change in the next decade, build the blocking, space, and entry in now.
